 Abstract

 Vision-Language-Action (VLA) foundation models have scaled rapidly to enhance manipulation performance and generalizability, but this scaling incurs high computational costs that render real-world deployment increasingly challenging.
Existing approaches typically mitigate this issue by designing smaller architectures or reducing the iterative denoising steps in flow-based policies.
In this work, we propose  FastOPD , a foundation-to-lightweight VLA framework that enables the practical deployment of large-scale VLAs through efficient on-policy distillation.
Specifically, FastOPD adapts a flow map for single-state teacher supervision and combines it with a self-consistency objective to construct a compact student that learns the teacher dynamics.
Furthermore, we theoretically demonstrate that minimizing this objective allows the distilled student to recover a distribution on par with that induced by an ideal few-step teacher model.
We evaluate FastOPD across diverse foundation policies in simulation and real-world experiments.
On LIBERO, FastOPD retains 84% of the performance of  π 0.5 \pi_{0.5}  with only two inference steps, reducing inference latency by 78.1% while outperforming existing few-step distillation baselines in average success rate. With LingBot-VLA as the teacher, FastOPD improves the single-step success rate over the base student by 15.9 percentage points on RoboTwin 2.0. We further demonstrate its applicability to a World Action Model (WAM) and deploy a compact student distilled from MolmoAct2 on a real robot.

 1  Introduction


 Recent progress in robot learning has moved toward large-scale foundation policies, ranging from Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models  ( Collaboration, 2024 ;  Black et al., 2025b ;  Black et al., 2025a ;  Bjorck et al., 2025 ;  Wu et al., 2026 ;  Fang et al., 2026a )  to emerging World Action Models (WAMs)  ( Kim et al., 2026 ;  Ye et al., 2026 ;  Yuan et al., 2026 ) . In particular, recent methods such as DreamZero  ( Ye et al., 2026 )  and Cosmos Policy  ( Kim et al., 2026 )  leverage pretrained video generation models to capture rich visual dynamics and achieve strong generalization and manipulation performance.
However, these powerful foundation priors also lead to increasingly large policy backbones and high computational cost, creating a tension between model capability and deployment efficiency on physical robots.
Lightweight VLA models such as SmolVLA  ( Shukor et al., 2025 )  address the efficiency side of this trade-off by directly designing smaller policies with less than 0.5B parameters.


 A complementary direction is to transfer the capability of a powerful foundation policy into a compact student through knowledge distillation.
Knowledge Distillation (KD)  ( Hinton et al., 2015 ;  Mansourian et al., 2025 )  has been widely studied for transferring the capability of a large teacher model into a smaller student. Specifically, On-Policy Distillation (OPD)  ( Agarwal et al., 2024 )  supervises the student using teacher predictions on its own generated trajectories, reducing distribution mismatch and compounding errors from offline distillation. OPD also provides dense supervision along the student trajectory, in contrast to the sparse rewards commonly used in reinforcement learning. While OPD has been actively explored in LLM post-training, recent works have extended it to diffusion and flow models  ( Li et al., 2026a ;  Fang et al., 2026b ) .
However, directly applying OPD to flow-based policies is computationally inefficient. It requires rolling out the student’s denoising trajectory and repeatedly evaluating the teacher at the visited timesteps, resulting in high training cost for large foundation teachers.


 In this work, we revisit few-step flow-map models through the lens of
on-policy distillation (OPD) and introduce  FastOPD , a
foundation-to-lightweight VLA framework for efficient policy distillation.
Our goal is to transfer the capabilities of a large-scale VLA into a
compact student while substantially reducing both training and inference
costs, without sacrificing policy performance
(see Fig.  1 ).
To reduce the training cost of conventional OPD, FastOPD combines
on-policy teacher supervision of the student’s velocity field with a
self-consistency objective  ( Frans et al., 2025 ) .
Rather than querying teacher supervision throughout an entire
generation trajectory, the self-consistency objective propagates local
velocity supervision to finite-interval shortcut transitions, as
illustrated in Fig.  2 .
This allows the student to approximate the teacher dynamics without
explicitly constructing or evaluating a teacher flow map over each
shortcut transition.
We further show theoretically that FastOPD implicitly recovers the
distribution induced by the teacher’s ideal shortcut model, providing a
distributional justification for few-step distillation.

 Importantly, the same self-consistency formulation naturally induces an
any-step flow map  ( Boffi et al., 2024 ;  Song et al., 2023 ;  Kim et al., 2024 ;  Geng et al., 2025 ;  Sabour et al., 2025 ) ,
allowing the distilled student to operate with substantially fewer
sampling steps at inference.
FastOPD therefore compresses the foundation VLA along two complementary
dimensions: model capacity and sampling complexity, enabling deployment
on resource-constrained, consumer-grade hardware.
The proposed formulation also enables stable training in the challenging
few-step regime without adversarial objectives or costly higher-order
derivative computations.
A concurrent work, VLA-OPD  ( Zhong et al., 2026 ) , also studies on-policy
distillation for VLA models, but primarily uses expert supervision to
improve policy performance.
In contrast, FastOPD focuses on transferring a foundation policy into a
lightweight few-step student.


 We evaluate FastOPD on two simulation benchmarks and one real-world
manipulation task, using diverse foundation teachers with 3B–6B
parameters  ( Black et al., 2025a ;  Yuan et al., 2026 ;  Fang et al., 2026a ) .
To maximize both architectural and inference efficiency, we adopt
SmolVLA as the lightweight student policy.
We compare against representative few-step distillation methods,
including CTM, DMD, and iMF.
Across these settings, FastOPD enables the compact student to recover up
to 84% of the performance of teacher models that are more than
 7.3 × 7.3\times  larger, using only one or two sampling steps.
This substantially reduces inference cost while retaining a large
fraction of the foundation policy’s performance.
To the best of our knowledge, FastOPD is the first OPD framework for
VLAs that jointly reduces both model size and the number of sampling
steps while retaining the capabilities of a large-scale foundation
policy.

While these models capture high-quality multimodal action distributions, accurate integration typically requires many denoising steps, motivating few-step distillation for faster inference.


 Vision-Language-Action Models.
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models  ( Collaboration, 2024 ;  Black et al., 2025b ;  Black et al., 2025a ;  Bjorck et al., 2025 ;  Wu et al., 2026 ;  Liu et al., 2024 ;  Octo Model Team et al., 2024 )  couple a pretrained vision-language backbone with an action decoding head to map an observation and a language instruction to a distribution over robot actions, most commonly via a flow or diffusion action expert  ( Chi et al., 2023 ;  Black et al., 2025b )  trained with flow matching loss. Recent foundation VLA models illustrate the scale required for strong multi-task generalization, typically exceeding 2B parameters and trained on over 10,000 hours of multimodal robot data spanning multiple embodiments  ( Black et al., 2025b ;  Black et al., 2025a ;  Wu et al., 2026 ) , but remain expensive to deploy.
Several works  ( Octo Model Team et al., 2024 ;  Shukor et al., 2025 ;  Wen et al., 2025 ;  Reuss et al., 2025 )  instead train smaller VLAs; SmolVLA  ( Shukor et al., 2025 ) , for example, reduces model size to 450 million parameters by using a small VLM and layer skipping.
However, it still requires  N = 10 N{=}10  denoising steps per action chunk and does not benefit from the capabilities of larger foundation policies.


 Flow Map Models.
A wide range of few-step generative methods aim to reduce iterative sampling cost.
Distillation-based approaches  ( Sabour et al., 2025 ;  Boffi et al., 2024 ) , including Consistency Models (CM)  ( Song et al., 2023 ) , and Consistency Trajectory Models (CTM)  ( Kim et al., 2024 ) , distill a pre-trained multi-step teacher into a few-step student, while score-distillation methods directly match teacher and student distributions  ( Yin et al., 2024b ;  Yin et al., 2024a ) .
Distillation-free approaches, such as MeanFlow (MF)  ( Geng et al., 2025 )  and Shortcut Models  ( Frans et al., 2025 ) , instead train from scratch using self-consistency constraints. A flow map model  𝒇 θ ​ ( 𝒙 , s , t ) {\bm{f}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}},s,t)  parameterizes the state-to-state transition over an arbitrary interval  [ s , t ] ⊂ [ 0 , 1 ] [s,t]\subset[0,1] , so that a single jump  𝒇 θ ​ ( 𝒙 s , s , t ) = 𝒙 s + ( t − s ) ​ 𝒖 θ ​ ( 𝒙 s , s , t ) {\bm{f}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{s},s,t)={\bm{x}}_{s}+(t-s){\bm{u}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{s},s,t)  at the interpolant  𝒙 s = ( 1 − s ) ​ 𝒙 0 + s ​ 𝒙 1 {\bm{x}}_{s}=(1-s){\bm{x}}_{0}+s{\bm{x}}_{1}  replaces an entire sequence of Euler substeps. For example,  Frans et al. (2025)  combines flow matching loss with the self-consistency constraint at the midpoint time  m = ( s + t ) / 2 m=({s+t})/{2} :

In robot manipulation, these ideas have been adapted to accelerate diffusion- and flow-based policies such as Diffusion Policy  ( Chi et al., 2023 ) . Consistency Policy (CP)  ( Prasad et al., 2024 )  adapts CTM  ( Kim et al., 2024 ) ; OneDP  ( Wang et al., 2025 )  and SDM Policy  ( Jia et al., 2024 )  use distribution matching for one-step action generation; and recent methods learn average velocity fields  ( Geng et al., 2025 ;  Geng et al., 2026 ;  Sheng et al., 2026 ;  Guo et al., 2026 )  or shortcut objectives  ( Frans et al., 2025 ;  Luan et al., 2026 ;  Yan et al., 2025 ;  Li et al., 2026b )  regularized by flow matching.
For instance, SnapFlow  ( Luan et al., 2026 )  constructs its target from two Euler steps but trains only on fixed endpoints, while OFP  ( Li et al., 2026b )  derives its target from nested sub-intervals with a self-guided regularizer.
Despite these advances, few-step policies often suffer from training instability and degraded quality as the number of denoising steps decreases. This trade-off is particularly critical in robot manipulation, where low latency must be achieved without sacrificing the precision and stability required for physical execution, and still remains insufficiently addressed  ( Shukor et al., 2025 ) .


 On-policy Distillation.
Given a pretrained teacher diffusion model, on-policy distillation (OPD) supervises the student to match the teacher’s one-step transition kernel  p T ( ⋅ ∣ 𝒙 t i ) p_{T}(\cdot\mid{\bm{x}}_{t_{i}})  along the student’s own rollout  𝒙 0 : N ∼ p S , θ {\bm{x}}_{0:N}\sim p_{S,\theta} , by minimizing the reverse KL objective  ( Li et al., 2026a ) . Since the student and teacher share the same diffusion coefficient, resulting in identical covariance, its per-step KL reduces to a closed-form squared distance between the means,  KL ( p S ∥ p T ) ∝ ∥ μ S ( 𝒙 t i ; θ ) − μ T ( 𝒙 t i ) ∥ 2 2 \mathrm{KL}(p_{S}\|p_{T})\propto\|\mu_{S}({\bm{x}}_{t_{i}};\theta)-\mu_{T}({\bm{x}}_{t_{i}})\|_{2}^{2} . Despite providing dense supervision, this objective necessitates evaluating the teacher at every single denoising step, resulting in computational inefficiency. FastOPD circumvents this training bottleneck by supervising the student at a single on-policy state per trajectory.
Formulated within a few-step flow map regime, the distilled policy is naturally equipped for real-time robot control.

 4  Experiments


 Our experiments span two multitask simulation benchmarks (Sec.  4.1 ) and one real-robot task (Sec.  4.2 ).
We freeze the vision–language backbone and fine-tune only the action expert and projections with the additional time projection layer described in Sec.  3 .
We evaluate each method using 1, 2, 4, and 10 denoising steps and compare it against the teacher and the initial student with 50 trials per task.
Full experimental details are provided in Appendix  B .


 4.1  Simulation Experiments


 Benchmarks.
We evaluate FastOPD on LIBERO  ( Liu et al., 2023 )  and RoboTwin 2.0  ( Chen et al., 2025 ) .
LIBERO evaluates single-arm manipulation across four standard evaluation suites—Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long—comprising 40 tasks in total.
RoboTwin 2.0  ( Chen et al., 2025 )  comprises 50 challenging bimanual manipulation tasks, with 2,500 demonstrations collected in clean scenes and 25,000 demonstrations collected under heavy scene randomization.
We report results in both clean and randomized settings on unseen environments.


 Baselines.
For simulation experiments, we compare our method with three representative approaches to few-step generation: Consistency Trajectory Model (CTM)  ( Kim et al., 2024 ) ,
Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD)  ( Yin et al., 2024b ;  Yin et al., 2024a ) ,
and improved Mean Flow (iMF)  ( Geng et al., 2026 ) .
Since iMF was originally formulated for training from scratch, we adapt it to
the distillation setting by replacing its self-referential instantaneous
velocity target with the teacher velocity.
Except for DMD, a one-step generator, all baselines are designed as the flow-map architecture.


 Teacher and Student Models.
We use SmolVLA  ( Shukor et al., 2025 )  as the student model on both benchmarks,
paired with  π 0.5 \pi_{0.5}   ( Black et al., 2025a )  on LIBERO, LingBot-VLA  ( Wu et al., 2026 )  and Fast-WAM  ( Yuan et al., 2026 )  on RoboTwin 2.0.
All models are initialized from public benchmark-specific checkpoints.

 Distilling a World Action Model.
Recent World Action Models (WAMs) build on pretrained video generation models and have emerged as an alternative to foundation VLAs  ( Kim et al., 2026 ;  Ye et al., 2026 ;  Yuan et al., 2026 ) . To examine whether FastOPD extends beyond VLA teachers, we distill Fast-WAM  ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) , a 6B-parameter video-based teacher, into the same 451M SmolVLA student with an identical training recipe.
As shown in Tab.  3 , the student distilled from Fast-WAM achieves a 50.5% average success rate at a single denoising step, 15.2 percentage points over the initial SmolVLA baseline (35.3%). Furthermore, it reaches 56.3% at two steps, successfully transferring knowledge despite the substantial differences in architecture and pretraining between the teacher and the student.
This demonstrates that FastOPD is a versatile approach that applies not only to VLAs but also to WAMs, since it only requires the teacher’s velocity field on the student’s own action trajectories, regardless of how the teacher is built.

 4.2  Real-World Experiments


 To validate its real-world applicability, we further design a real-world robot scenario,  pnp-plate , in which the robot picks up the ball and places it on the plate.
Experiments use the right arm of a bimanual YAM robot with a parallel-jaw
gripper and top-view and wrist-mounted cameras that provide RGB observations.
We train MolmoAct2  ( Fang et al., 2026a ) , an action reasoning model that is suitable for real-world deployment, on our collected demonstrations for 14,000 iterations and use the resulting policy as the teacher.
We initialize the student from SmolVLA, warm it up on the collected demonstrations with
the standard flow matching objective (i.e., behavior cloning) for 10,000 iterations,
and subsequently distill the teacher into the student using FastOPD. Full experimental details
are provided in Appendix  B.5 .


 Fig.  6  and Fig.  6  summarize the results.
With four denoising steps, FastOPD achieves a 50% success rate, improving by 8 percentage points over
the 42% of the base SmolVLA at the same number of steps.
Notably, FastOPD shows faster and more accurate performance than the 10-step base student, improving the success rate from 44% to 50% while cutting the execution time from 19.32 s to 17.38 s.
These results demonstrate that FastOPD can transfer the capability of a larger teacher to a
compact student deployed on a physical robot with few steps.



 4.3  Ablation Study


 Training efficiency.
FastOPD reduces training overhead by supervising the student at a single sampled on-policy state rather than across all denoising steps.
As illustrated in Fig.  1 , standard OPD  ( Li et al., 2026a )  takes 9.51 hours per 1,000 training iterations while FastOPD requires only 1.43 hours, achieving a  5.7 × 5.7\times  speedup.
This translates directly into accelerated convergence (Fig.  1 ). FastOPD achieves a 78.2% 1-step success rate in just 10 hours, whereas conventional OPD requires roughly 57 hours to reach a similar level (79.1%). Consequently, FastOPD matches the performance of conventional OPD at a fraction of the computational cost.


 Student Initialization.

We study whether FastOPD can also start from a student that is initialized
in a fully on-policy manner. To do so, we initialize the student using 5,000 iterations of conventional per-step OPD  ( Li et al., 2026a )  instead of flow-matching behavior cloning.
As shown in Tab.  4 , the OPD-initialized student degrades severely at 4 steps (44.2%), likely due to the fixed 10-step discretization in OPD, whereas FastOPD recovers this case and improves all denoising steps.
This indicates that OPD is a viable alternative to
flow-matching-based behavior cloning for the initialization, although it incurs a substantially higher
training cost, as discussed above (Fig.  1 ). It also shows that FastOPD is not tied to a specific initialization
and can be applied on top of students distilled by other methods.

While  Luan et al. (2026)  also anchors a fixed two-step Euler target with flow matching, FastOPD anchors to the teacher via OPFD and enforces consistency over arbitrary intervals, yielding an any-step flow map that inherits the teacher’s dynamics (see Appendix  C ).

 Hyperparameters.
The proposed method introduces a single
hyperparameter  λ \lambda , which controls the strength of the OPFD
term. To study its effect, we sweep the OPFD weight  λ ∈ { 1 , 0.5 , 0.1 , 0.01 } \lambda\in\{1,0.5,0.1,0.01\}  on the OPD-initialized student (Tab.  6 ).
Performance collapses at  λ = 0.01 \lambda=0.01 , indicating that sufficient OPFD supervision is needed to anchor the self-consistency term, and remains stable within  [ 0.1 , 0.5 ] [0.1,0.5]  with a mild trade-off between few- and multi-step performance.


 Action execution steps.  The policy executes the first  K K  actions of each predicted chunk before replanning, trading responsiveness for inference overhead. Short execution horizons
improve responsiveness through more frequent replanning, while longer horizons reduce inference overhead at the cost of extended open-loop execution. As shown in Fig.  7 , we observe that both the teacher  π 0.5 \pi_{0.5}  and the student perform best at  K = 10 K=10 , suggesting that the student inherits the teacher’s preferred replanning frequency.




 5  Conclusion


 In this paper, we present FastOPD, an efficient on-policy distillation framework that transfers large-scale VLAs into lightweight student models to maximize computational efficiency without compromising performance.
Specifically, FastOPD streamlines the iterative denoising process by anchoring a single on-policy state to the teacher, coupled with a self-consistency objective for finite-interval transitions.
In simulation and real-robot experiments across diverse VLA and WAM teachers, FastOPD improves over the base student in the few-step regime and outperforms existing few-step distillation methods, while substantially reducing training and inference cost. We hope this framework facilitates scalable, real-time deployment of foundation policies on physical robots.

 B.5  Real-world Experimental Details


 Robot setup.
We follow the real-robot setup of MolmoAct2  ( Fang et al., 2026a ) . Specifically, we use the right arm of a bimanual YAM robot with six joints and a parallel-jaw
gripper, controlled by absolute joint-position commands (7-dimensional action space), with the
joint velocity capped at 0.75 rad/s (Fig.  8 ).
The policy observes two RGB views (top and right wrist;  480 × 640 480\times 640 , 30 fps; current frame
only) together with the 7-dimensional proprioceptive state (six joints and gripper), and receives
the language instruction.
Actions and states are normalized with quantile normalization for all models (the gripper is not normalized for MolmoAct2). All policies are run on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090, with SmolVLA-based
models in float32.

 Connection with previous works.
Although SnapFlow  ( Luan et al., 2026 )  adopts a two-step consistency target similar to ours, the two objectives differ in both their anchor and their scope.
SnapFlow is a self-distillation method that combines flow matching on ground-truth actions with a shortcut loss defined only on the fixed interval from  t = 1 t=1  to  t = 0 t=0 :




 ℒ SnapFlow = λ 1 ​ ℒ FM + ( 1 − λ 1 ) ​ λ 2 ​ ℒ shortcut , ℒ shortcut = ‖ 𝒖 θ ​ ( 𝒙 1 , 1 , 0 ∣ c ) − 𝒗 tgt ‖ 2 2 , \mathcal{L}_{\text{SnapFlow}}=\lambda_{1}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}+(1-\lambda_{1})\lambda_{2}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{shortcut}},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\text{shortcut}}=\big\|{\bm{u}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{1},1,0\mid c)-{\bm{v}}_{\text{tgt}}\big\|_{2}^{2},

 (41)

 where  𝒗 tgt = 1 2 ​ [ sg ⁡ [ 𝒗 θ ​ ( 𝒙 1 , 1 ∣ c ) ] + sg ⁡ [ 𝒗 θ ​ ( 𝒙 ^ 0.5 , 0.5 ∣ c ) ] ] {\bm{v}}_{\text{tgt}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\mathrm{sg}[{\bm{v}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{1},1\mid c)]+\mathrm{sg}[{\bm{v}}_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0.5},0.5\mid c)]\big]  and  𝒙 ^ 0.5 = 𝒙 1 − 0.5 ​ sg ​ [ 𝒗 θ ​ ( 𝒙 1 , 1 ∣ c ) ] \hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0.5}={\bm{x}}_{1}-0.5\,\mathrm{sg}[{\bm{v}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{1},1\mid c)] .
In contrast, FastOPD enforces self-consistency over arbitrary intervals  ( s , t ) (s,t) , yielding a flow map that supports any number of sampling steps, and anchors the target to the teacher through OPFD rather than to the data through flow matching.
SnapFlow can thus be viewed as a teacher-free counterpart of our objective restricted to a single interval, which allows us to isolate the contribution of teacher supervision.

 D.1  Distribution Matching Distillation


 Referring to OneDP  ( Wang et al., 2025 ) , we design DMD as a one-step generator, and jointly train it with a fake-score critic  𝒗 ψ ​ ( 𝒙 , t ) {\bm{v}}_{\psi}({\bm{x}},t)
of the same architecture, initialized from the same checkpoint as

 Generator.  A sample is produced with a single Euler step of the
student’s own PF-ODE from pure noise  𝒙 1 ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 𝟎 , 𝐈 ) {\bm{x}}_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}) ,




 𝒙 ^ 0 = 𝒙 1 − 𝒗 θ ​ ( 𝒙 1 , 1 ) . \hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}={\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{1},1).

 From the re-noised sample  𝒙 t {\bm{x}}_{t}  from  𝒙 ^ 0 \hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0} , the teacher and critic denoised estimates are formed as

 MeanFlow  ( Geng et al., 2025 )  parameterizes the average velocity
 𝒖 ⁡ ( 𝒙 , s , t ) := 1 s − t ​ ∫ t s 𝒗 ⁡ ( 𝒙 τ , τ ) ​ 𝑑 τ {\bm{u}}({\bm{x}},s,t):=\frac{1}{s-t}\int_{t}^{s}{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}_{\tau},\tau)\,d\tau  and train it
through the identity obtained by differentiating the definition along  s s ,




 𝒖 ⁡ ( 𝒙 s , s , t ) = 𝒗 ⁡ ( 𝒙 s , s ) − ( s − t ) ​ d d ​ s ​ 𝒖 ​ ( 𝒙 s , s , t ) , d d ​ s ​ 𝒖 = ∂ 𝒙 𝒖 ⋅ 𝒗 ⁡ ( 𝒙 s , s ) + ∂ s 𝒖 . {\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}_{s},s,t)={\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}_{s},s)-(s-t)\,\frac{d}{ds}{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}_{s},s,t),\qquad\frac{d}{ds}{\bm{u}}=\partial_{\bm{x}}{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}_{s},s)+\partial_{s}{\bm{u}}.

 (51)

 In the original formulation,  𝒗 ⁡ ( 𝒙 s , s ) {\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}_{s},s)  in
Eq. ( 51 ) is taken to be the student’s own
instantaneous velocity  𝒗 θ ​ ( 𝒙 s , s ) := 𝒖 θ ​ ( 𝒙 s , s , s ) {\bm{v}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{s},s):={\bm{u}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{s},s,s) , so
the regression target is itself computed by the network being
optimized.
Improved MeanFlow (iMF)  ( Geng et al., 2026 )  addresses this issue by
reformulating the identity to remove this self-reference.


 We follow this reformulation and further adapt it to distillation by replacing the self-referential term with the frozen teacher velocity,
 𝒗 ϕ ​ ( 𝒙 s , s ) {\bm{v}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{s},s) , used both as the target and
the tangent direction of the total derivative.


 Given

Jacobian–Vector Product (JVP) of  𝒖 θ {\bm{u}}_{\theta}  at  ( 𝒙 s , s , t ) ({\bm{x}}_{s},s,t)  along the
tangent  ( 𝒗 ϕ , 1 , 0 ) ({\bm{v}}_{\phi},1,0) :

 (53)
